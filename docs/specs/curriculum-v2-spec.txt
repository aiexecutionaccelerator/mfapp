
Mission Fragrances mission revision specification
Implementation handoff for Kai Hegde
Prepared for Antonio Centeno  |  28 October 2026  |  Curriculum revision 2
The change to make
Revise the 30 missions into a deliberate progression that helps a man practice honor, courage, and commitment. Keep the original app structure and presentation. Each mission must have a distinct purpose, a manageable task, useful options, an honest completion rule, and a clear connection to the missions around it.
The intended outcome is greater self-trust, confidence, focus, and follow-through: a man can look at his own actions and see evidence that he keeps his word, faces worthwhile discomfort, and returns to his priorities. These are product goals to evaluate with users, not guaranteed psychological outcomes.
What Kai should implement
Port the exact 30 mission definitions in this document into the existing Mission Fragrances app, preserving mission numbers and the original visual system.
Use Select, Trigger, Act, Record throughout. Trigger is spraying the fragrance. Recalling a past experience belongs under Act and is recorded as a reflection.
Carry the user’s own code, promise, aim, and practice into later missions. Keep examples as guidance; never save example text as the user’s evidence.
Distinguish a completed action from a saved reflection, code statement, plan, and unfinished draft. Preserve existing user records and progress during the change.
Implement and test on a reviewable branch or staging environment. Return a walkthrough, migration evidence, and the completed acceptance checklist before a live release.
Scope and status
The revised curriculum and supporting screens exist in a private prototype. This specification is the implementation contract for carrying that work into the existing app. The prototype is a reference, not a replacement production backend. Its source, known gaps, and prior checks are identified later in this document.
Keep the original four tabs, narrow phone layout, scent selection, vertical STAR steps, full-width primary button, completion screen, Proof Log, and Personal Code. Preserve the existing STAR explanation and video section. Kai reported that section restored on 28 October; confirm it remains intact during this revision.
No redesign of authentication, hosting, payments, push notifications, or the production database is requested. Do not introduce public journals, leaderboards, mandatory sharing, calendar locks, or a reset after a missed day.

The connections that make the sequence work
Missions 1–5 create small experiences of the three values before the user is asked to define them. Mission 6 adds a specific remembered example. Mission 7 attaches the practice to an event already present in his day. Missions 8–12 convert this experience into standards and a meaningful promise.
Mission 13 turns the code into an identity direction. Mission 14 immediately tests a code line through behavior. Missions 15–20 exercise distinct practical skills; the saved promise stays visible so the tasks have a personal reference point. They need not all advance the same work or financial goal: caring for relationships, capacity, and other people remains part of the curriculum.
Mission 21 is the deliberate narrowing point. The user chooses an area and a specific seven-day aim. Missions 22–25 must use that saved aim: take an avoided step, prepare the next repetition, protect time, then remove a recurring distraction. Mission 26 teaches a repair or restart. Mission 27 uses experience to design a repeatable practice with a fallback.
Mission 28 repeats something that worked. Mission 29 explains a selected standard and its practical behavior to a trusted person. Mission 30 reviews actual evidence, keeps or adjusts the practice, and defines how to continue for the next month.
Why repetition remains purposeful
Mission 2 practices starting; Mission 17 practices sustained attention; Mission 22 applies courage to a selected aim. Mission 7 establishes the fragrance cue; Mission 19 removes a physical obstacle; Mission 23 prepares a specific next repetition; Mission 25 changes a recurring distraction. Mission 3 keeps a promise now; Mission 12 defines a continuing promise; Mission 27 turns it into a repeatable plan. Preserve these differences in copy and examples.
Pacing and agency
Recommend one mission a day and always identify the next unfinished mission by number. Let the user browse all 30 and continue sooner if useful. Only genuine prerequisites block completion. A missed day does not remove progress, reset the sequence, or imply failure. Thirty missions are the program length, not a promise that a habit forms in 30 days.

How to interpret Act
The general STAR explanation includes reflection, but individual missions still have a defined assignment. Remembering a past action does not complete an action mission. Mission 6 is explicitly a reflection. Missions 8–13 define code or identity. Missions 21, 27, and 30 save plans. Completion wording must identify the actual outcome.
Options and examples
Under Act, use the expandable label “Choose one way to do it” and helper “Pick the option that fits your life, or use your own version of the same task.” The three options are equivalent approaches to one objective. They are not a scored quiz, required radio buttons, or three tasks to complete.
Under Record, use the expandable label “Need an example?” and helper “For inspiration. Write what is true for you.” Examples are explanatory text. Tapping or expanding them must never populate the record, check the completion box, or save anything.
Mission 13 is the intentional exception to a blank starting field: show the current identity statement, or “Keeps his word, acts despite fear, and follows through.” The user must deliberately keep or edit it and save. Merely visiting the screen never completes the mission.
Suggested value definitions
Honor: “You live by a moral code, tell the truth, keep promises, and own your actions.” Courage: “You do what you know is right despite fear, danger, or pressure to do otherwise.” Commitment: “You fulfill your responsibilities through consistent effort and perseverance.” Keep these shared definitions consistent; the mission rationale explains their particular application.

Labels and confirmation

Existing records use “SAVE CHANGES”. All unfinished missions offer “SAVE DRAFT FOR LATER”.
The shared success headline may say “MISSION COMPLETE.” The supporting line must remain type-specific: “You acted with [selected value].”, “Your reflection is saved.”, “Your Personal Code is taking shape.”, “Your focus and aim are saved.”, or “Your practice plan is saved.” A code or plan must never be praised as a completed external behavior.

Context rules for production
Use the user’s own saved wording. Display promise, focus, aim, and practice as contextual information under Act, not as suggested answers. The focus areas are Relationships, Health, Work, Money, Home, and Service. A focus area alone is not a seven-day aim: Mission 21 also requires the user’s own result and reason in the text field.
Capture the task version and the relevant context used for each completed record. Editing a later goal must never rewrite what an older action was attempting. Preserve the original completion date; use a separate update timestamp for edits. Completed context is historical and remains frozen.
Unfinished drafts need different handling. If the prerequisite was absent when the draft began, show the newly saved context when it becomes available. If the user changes an aim while a later draft exists, show that the aim changed and let him keep the previous aim or switch to the current one. Keep his draft text in either case. Persist the chosen context on completion.
Known prototype differences to resolve
The prototype currently freezes context at the first draft save, including an empty context. A Mission 22 draft created before Mission 21 can therefore miss the subsequently saved aim. Its practice panel is also nested under the aim panel, so a valid Mission 27 practice can be hidden when no Mission 21 aim exists. Fix both conditions when implementing this spec.
The prototype derives the five code lines live. Production should additionally snapshot the code context used by Missions 14 and 29, so a later code edit does not change the meaning of the original action. Show current standards for unfinished work and the historical version for completed records.
Removal and exceptions
Removing a prerequisite blocks future completion but preserves downstream history. Restore reinstates the same record. Explain missing prerequisites without destroying text. Browsing, drafts, and completed records remain available; do not lock every mission behind all earlier missions.

Validation at the trusted write boundary
Derive kind and task definition from the server’s approved mission version. Reject a client attempting to reclassify a plan or reflection as an action, or replace its mission snapshot.
Require Honor, Courage, or Commitment as the selected fragrance. Require trimmed, nonempty record text; maximum 1,000 characters, or 280 for Mission 13. Profile name maximum is 60 characters.
Require action confirmation on completed action saves. Validate prerequisites on the server as well as in the interface. Incomplete fields may be saved in drafts without progress credit.
For 27 and 30 require Daily, Weekly, or Three times a week, plus a real future review date in the user’s timezone. Reject impossible dates and new dates today or earlier. Allow an unchanged historical review date when editing a previously saved plan.
Optional photos are supported only on 7 and 19. Preserve production upload limits and authorization; do not fetch arbitrary external photo URLs. Photo upload failure must not erase text or falsely show a successful save.
Save integrity
Use one canonical completed record per mission per user. Repeated save clicks and network retries must update that record, not duplicate it. One-Off Actions need unique independent IDs. The prototype uses mission-N and extra-UUID within its single-owner journal; production IDs or uniqueness constraints must also include the user boundary.
Use the existing conflict or revision mechanism to prevent lost writes. Show success only after confirmed persistence. On validation or network failure, keep text and explain the error beside the form. Save draft, complete, edit, remove, restore, and reload must produce consistent progress, log, code, and identity views.
The app cannot prove that an offline action happened or judge whether a personal standard is sincere. Clear instructions, honest labels, and explicit user confirmation are the intended safeguards. Do not add automatic tense grading or AI scoring as a release requirement.
Migration and production release
Preserve the meaning of old records
Before replacing production mission definitions, capture the actual live definitions and their version mapping. Archive or snapshot each historical task so an old record continues to show the instructions under which it was created. Do not assume the prototype’s legacy-missions.json represents every production version.
For example, earlier Mission 4 was a gratitude task. A record saying “I thanked my partner” must remain attached to that original task. It must not be relabeled “Own Your Part” or presented as evidence of an apology. Show an earlier-wording notice and allow the revised task to be viewed separately.
Keep mission numbers stable and existing completion credit intact. Existing users are not required to redo revised missions. Preserve old drafts under their original wording, with an explicit choice if offering a switch to the revised task. Never silently replace the task associated with an existing draft or completion.
Classify legacy Mission 21 records carefully: the old focus choice may lack a seven-day aim. Keep the old completion, but ask the user to add a current aim before starting revised Missions 22–25. Do not invent an aim from a category or relabel a historical record as if it already contained one.
Implementation order
Inventory the current production branch, schema, mission definitions, STAR/video placements, and existing behavior. Record the baseline and differences from this spec.
Create a reviewable branch. Add versioned curriculum data and migration support before changing displayed instructions.
Implement the shared mission renderer, truthful type labels, context propagation, draft refresh, prerequisites, and validation. Preserve the existing visual system and account architecture.
Run the acceptance scenarios on clean accounts and representative legacy accounts, including interrupted and conflicting saves. Verify original and revised task histories side by side.
Provide staging access or a walkthrough, a changed-files summary, test results, migration counts, known issues, and a rollback plan. Antonio reviews wording and experience before live rollout.
Safe rollback behavior
Back up relevant production records before migration. Make the migration idempotent and report counts for mapped, unchanged, and unresolved records. Unresolved version mapping must remain visible for review, not silently assigned. A release toggle or rollback must preserve newly written revision 2 records and snapshots; rolling back UI code must not discard or mislabel their data.
Production boundary
The Sites prototype is an owner-private journal using a single-owner storage pattern. Do not grant team-wide or public access to that journal or reuse its account boundary for customers. Kai should use the existing production authentication, user isolation, storage, and notification services. Review copies and synthetic test accounts should contain no private journal content.
What has and has not been checked
The prototype has passed TypeScript checks, a production build, 18 state checks, and browser inspection of all 30 mission screens, drafts, action confirmation, and the Mission 21 to 22 handoff. These are reference checks, not a substitute for testing the production port. They do not establish a completed 30-day study, improved confidence, retention, real-device reliability, or a completed production migration.

Design background and limits
Specific starting cues and planned responses are informed by Gollwitzer and Sheeran, Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement, 2006: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1 . Repetition in context and a practical return after missed opportunities are informed by Lally and colleagues, How Are Habits Formed, 2010: https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674 .
These sources inform the design; they do not validate this curriculum or a special fragrance effect. Evaluate task clarity, meaningful action, return after interruptions, and usefulness. Completion counts alone do not establish confidence or habit formation.
